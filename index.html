What this is

In September 2026, an MIT team (Levitt, Lewis et al., Science Translational Medicine) played 50 ms bursts of pink noise to 14 sleeping adults. The bursts were timed to land on the peak of each slow brain wave in deep sleep, and they made those waves, and the flow of brain fluid (CSF) that goes with them, stronger.

What this app can't do

The timing was the whole trick. They read the brain waves live with EEG and fired the sound at the right moment. A phone can't see your brain waves, so this app can't hit the peaks, and it can't know when you're actually in deep sleep. The researchers themselves say there's no evidence plain continuous pink noise does the same thing, and another study found all-night steady noise cut REM sleep.

What it does instead

  • Short, soft 50 ms pink noise bursts, same sound as the study.
  • Only in the first hours after you fall asleep, where most deep sleep usually sits.
  • Rhythm: groups of 5 bursts at about 0.8 per second, the pace of slow waves (older no-EEG studies used rhythms like this, with weaker, mixed results). Pairs: 2 bursts, then a long rest, closer to the study's sparse style.
  • Then it goes silent and stops by itself, so the REM-heavy morning is left alone.

So treat it like

A fun self-experiment. Blind test mode helps you tell a real effect from a good-mood effect. If a burst ever wakes you, turn it down. Not medical advice, not a cure for anything.

Later

There's a hook for an EEG headband. With one, the app could do the real closed-loop thing.
